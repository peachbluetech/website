Amazon DSP still credits a conversion to the most relevant click or viewable impression in the 14 days before it, but since January 1, 2026 that rule no longer covers every number in your report. Views on DSP ads served in Amazon's store now go through a shopping-signal model with a shorter window, the old count moved to separate "all views" metrics, and Amazon's new reporting books conversions on a different date than it used to.

So a DSP ROAS that fell in January may not mean the media got worse. Below: the window now, what each DSP metric measures, and a weekly way to read them, with definitions from Amazon's support center and Ads API documentation, read in October 2026.

## What is the Amazon DSP attribution window now?

By default, 14 days for both clicks and views, on a last-touch model that ranks clicks above views and promoted products above brand halo (Amazon Ads support center, "Ad campaign attribution"). Each conversion is credited once across all campaigns and all advertisers, so DSP competes with your Sponsored Brands and Display campaigns for the same sale. A view counts only if the impression was viewable: at least half the ad on screen for one second for display, two seconds for video.

The default now has exceptions, so the window depends on where the ad ran and how the campaign is set up:

| Conversion | Window and model | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Click on any DSP ad | 14 days, last touch | Standard metrics |
| View of a DSP ad served outside Amazon's store | 14 days, last touch | Standard metrics |
| View of a DSP ad served in Amazon's store, since January 1, 2026 | Shopping-signal model, shorter window (Amazon publishes no length) | Standard metrics |
| The same store views under the old rule | 14 days, every eligible view | "All views" metrics, such as Purchases (all views) |
| Any interaction on a campaign using flexible look-back | The click and view windows the advertiser selected | Standard metrics |

The last row is easy to miss. Amazon's reporting documentation says DSP campaigns using flexible look-back windows report the click and view windows the advertiser chose, and Amazon does not publish the list of choices, so check the campaign's settings before you treat a number as a 14-day number.

## What changed in Amazon DSP attribution on January 1, 2026?

Amazon moved view attribution for store-served ads to what it calls a shopping-signal enhanced last-touch model. The model gives credit to ad views that reach shoppers in discovery moments, such as exploratory browsing or general category searches, and applies a shorter window. It covers DSP campaigns that serve ads in the Amazon store, plus Sponsored Brands and viewable-CPM Sponsored Display. Click attribution did not change, and neither did any other inventory (Amazon Ads launch announcement, January 1, 2026).

Three things follow for anyone reading DSP reports:

- **The standard metrics moved.** Purchases, sales, and ROAS use the new model for affected views, and Amazon recommends the standard metrics for ongoing performance.
- **The old numbers did not disappear.** The original model, every eligible view in 14 days, lives on as "all views" metrics in Amazon's unified reporting, its report builder, and the API.
- **Year over year needs care.** 2025 standard metrics were counted under the old rule. Compare them with 2026 all-views figures, never with 2026 standard figures.

A worked example, with illustrative numbers. A remarketing line spends $10,000 in a month of 2026:

| Metric | Standard | All views |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchases | 310 | 400 |
| Sales | $18,600 | $24,000 |
| ROAS | 1.86 | 2.40 |

The view attribution gap is (400 minus 310) divided by 400, or 22.5 percent: almost a quarter of the purchases the old rule credited to this line no longer qualify. If the line showed a 2.40 ROAS in 2025, its performance did not change; the rule did. The gap is also information: lines that lose the most credit leaned hardest on passive views of shoppers already in the store, which makes them the first candidates for a holdout test.

Peachblue's composite score is a percentile blend of CTR, ROAS, CPA, and spend, so it ranks creatives against each other rather than against a fixed number. A rule change that lowers every line together does not demote a creative on its own; where tiers do reshuffle, the change hit some lines harder than others, and that is worth a look.

## Which Amazon DSP metrics measure delivery?

Delivery metrics say whether the money bought ads that could be seen, and at what real price. Read them first, because every rate further down divides by them.

| Metric | How Amazon calculates it | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impressions | Gross impressions minus invalid impressions | What you paid for after traffic-quality filtering |
| Invalid impression rate | Invalid impressions / gross impressions | How much was filtered out |
| Measurable rate | Measurable impressions / impressions | How much could be checked for viewability at all |
| Viewable rate | Viewable impressions / measurable impressions | Of what could be checked, how much was seen |
| Viewable distribution | Viewable impressions / impressions | Of everything bought, how much was seen |
| CPM | Total cost / impressions x 1,000 | Price per thousand impressions |
| vCPM | Total cost / viewable impressions x 1,000 | Price per thousand impressions that were seen |

A worked example. A line buys 1,000,000 impressions at an $8 CPM, so $8,000 of cost. 800,000 were measurable and 560,000 viewable: an 80 percent measurable rate, a 70 percent viewable rate, a 56 percent viewable distribution, and a vCPM of $14.29. If next month the CPM holds at $8 but viewable distribution falls to 45 percent, the vCPM rises to $17.78 while the invoice looks flat.

One agency detail: in Amazon's new reporting, total cost excludes the third-party fees the agency pays, so check which cost column your invoice uses before you compute CPMs or margin. Peachblue applies your agency margin per client to spend and spend-derived metrics, so the client sees billable numbers while pace is still measured against the raw budget.

## How do you read Amazon DSP video and streaming metrics?

Video metrics measure attention, and on streaming TV they are most of what you have, because there is usually nothing to click. Amazon reports starts, the plays that reached 25, 50, and 75 percent (first quartile, midpoint, third quartile), and complete views. Completion rate is complete views divided by starts; cost per completed view is total cost divided by complete views.

A worked example. 200,000 starts and 120,000 complete views is a 60 percent completion rate, and at $6,000 of cost that is $0.05 per completed view. Then read the shape: if 90 percent of starts reach the first quartile but only 60 percent finish, the opening holds and the back half loses people, which is an edit question, not a media question. Only compare completion between spots of the same length.

Read these per creative, not per line item, because the same video usually runs in several line items and a ranking by row splits its results. ## Which Amazon DSP metrics show shopping behavior?

On-Amazon conversion metrics are what make DSP different from other programmatic buying: you can watch shoppers move toward a product long before purchases pile up. All of them follow the attribution rules above.

| Metric | What it counts | Read it as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Detail page views | Product page visits attributed to the ad | Consideration |
| Add to cart | Cart adds attributed to the ad | Purchase intent |
| Branded searches | Searches for the promoted brand attributed to the ad | Awareness turning into intent |
| Purchases and sales | Attributed orders and their value | The outcome |
| ROAS | Sales / total cost | Efficiency, for lines meant to sell |

Two definitions catch people out in 2026:

- **Halo is in the base number.** In Amazon's new Reporting API, generally available since September 30, 2026, "purchases" includes both promoted and halo products, the figure DSP used to call total purchases. Halo means other products from the same brand, which the help center limits to the promoted product's subcategory. Use the promoted-only metric when a client asks what the advertised product sold.
- **Views do most of the work.** Amazon's help center says view-through conversions are typically the majority for DSP campaigns, which is why the January change matters so much more here than on click-only ad types.

For what ROAS can and cannot tell you, see [what ROAS is and where it misleads](/blog/what-is-roas). In Peachblue, ask Agent Peach, or Claude over the MCP server, to rank last month's DSP creatives and the answer comes back grouped by creative and scored, with spend beside each, not as rows by line item.

## How do you read new-to-brand and long-term sales?

New-to-brand (NTB) separates the customers a campaign recruited from the ones it reconverted. A purchase is new to brand when the shopper had not bought from the brand in the previous 12 months, and Amazon reports NTB purchases, NTB sales, NTB ROAS, and the percent of purchases new to brand (NTB purchases divided by purchases). Two lines with the same ROAS and different NTB shares are doing different jobs: one grows the customer base, the other harvests it.

Long-term sales carries the same idea forward. Amazon defines it as attributed sales plus the estimated 365-day value of the new-to-brand shoppers a campaign brought in, based on the brand's historical shopping patterns, and divides it by cost for long-term ROAS. It is an estimate, not a count, so label it that way in a client deck and keep the attributed number beside it.

Both matter most for upper-funnel lines, which is where a single ROAS figure misleads hardest. ## Why do Amazon DSP conversions keep changing after the fact?

Because the window is still open. Amazon's new reporting books every conversion on the date of the ad interaction, so a sale on October 14 that followed a view on October 1 lands in the October 1 row, and a report date stays incomplete until its look-back ends. Conversions can take up to 12 hours to appear, and orders cancelled within 72 hours are deducted, which can take up to 7 days. Older DSP reports booked conversions on the purchase date instead, so the same campaign can show a different daily curve depending on which report you pulled.

So treat the last two weeks of conversion data as still filling in, and never compare a closed week with an open one. Delivery-side signals do not wait: Peachblue's fatigue board flags CTR decay against each creative's own best rolling week, which needs no attribution window to settle and is most useful on display, where people click.

## How should you compare Amazon DSP with Meta and Google?

Label the window and model on every platform column, and compare each channel with its own history rather than head to head. A DSP column counts Amazon store activity on Amazon's last-touch click-and-view rules; Meta and Google default to different windows and count different events, so equal ROAS does not mean equal performance. For a fuller picture inside Amazon, multi-touch metrics (launched in beta for US advertisers) split credit across the Amazon ads a shopper saw, and Amazon Marketing Cloud allows custom look-backs up to 28 days with other attribution models. The [DSP reporting guide](/blog/amazon-dsp-reporting-guide) covers the report surfaces themselves.
